Miyan Gyada: The Groundnut Soup That Turns Peanuts Into a Full Meal

By Yvonne Aizle·September 6, 2026

A handful of groundnuts can be eaten as a snack. Grind them, simmer them with peppers, stock, and greens, and they become the body of an entire meal.

That transformation is at the heart of miyan gyada, a groundnut soup associated with Hausa cooking and northern Nigeria. Peanuts provide its creamy texture and deep flavor, while meat, dried fish, fermented seasoning, and leafy vegetables can make the pot even more substantial. Recipes change between households: some cooks roast the groundnuts before grinding them, others use them raw, and one version may cling to tuwo while another stays loose enough to serve over rice. The dish has a recognizable foundation, but no single recipe represents every Hausa kitchen.

What is miyan gyada?

Miyan gyada is a Hausa soup made by simmering ground peanuts in seasoned liquid until it thickens. Depending on the household, it may contain beef, goat, chicken, dried fish, vegetables, tomatoes, peppers, oil, or daddawa, a fermented locust bean seasoning.

The name is direct: miya means soup or sauce, while gyaɗa means groundnut or peanut. The fuller Hausa form is miyar gyaɗa, since the feminine noun miya takes a connecting "-r." Miyan gyada, miyan geda, and miyar gyada are all common spellings in English writing. The dish belongs to a wider West African family of peanut soups, but its seasoning, consistency, and traditional pairings reflect Hausa and northern Nigerian cooking specifically.

Why groundnuts matter in northern Nigerian cooking

Groundnuts can be roasted, pressed for oil, made into ƙuli-ƙuli, blended into kunun gyaɗa, or used to thicken soup. Their farming and trade also shaped northern Nigeria's twentieth-century economy, symbolized by Kano's stacked groundnut pyramids. In miyan gyada, the peanuts thicken the liquid, carry the seasoning, and add protein and fat, while the finished meal's nutritional value depends on whatever meat, fish, vegetables, oil, and starch are served alongside it.

What goes into miyan gyada

Raw peanuts create a milder base, while gently toasted nuts add deeper flavor. Unsweetened natural peanut butter can stand in when whole groundnuts aren't available.

  • Groundnuts: ground into a powder or paste
  • Beef, goat, or chicken: cooked until tender, stock reserved
  • Dried or smoked fish: added for savory depth
  • Onions, peppers, and tomatoes: the seasoning base
  • Daddawa: fermented locust beans, concentrated and earthy
  • Palm oil or groundnut oil: a matter of preference
  • Leafy vegetables: often spinach, sorrel, or another available green
  • Ginger, garlic, crayfish, bouillon, and salt: to taste

The recipe is flexible, but the liquid needs attention: ground peanuts keep thickening as they cook and cool, so the soup may need extra stock or water along the way.

How to make miyan gyada

This follows the broad structure found across several northern Nigerian and Hausa-style versions. Treat it as an adaptable starting point, not the only correct method.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups raw, skinned groundnuts
  • 700 grams beef, goat, or chicken (optional)
  • 1 small dried or smoked fish, cleaned and deboned (optional)
  • 1 large onion
  • 2 red bell peppers
  • 1 or 2 Scotch bonnet peppers
  • 2 medium tomatoes or 1 tablespoon tomato paste (optional)
  • 2 tablespoons palm oil or groundnut oil
  • 1 to 2 teaspoons ground daddawa (optional)
  • 1 teaspoon grated ginger
  • 2 garlic cloves
  • 4 cups meat stock, vegetable stock, or water
  • 3 cups chopped spinach or other tender greens
  • Salt and seasoning to taste

Method

  1. Cook the meat with some onion, ginger, garlic, salt, and enough water to cover it. Reserve the stock. Keep dried fish aside until later so it doesn't break apart.
  2. For a roasted flavor, toast the groundnuts over low heat, stirring constantly. Cool completely, then grind into a fine powder or paste.
  3. Blend the remaining onion with the peppers and tomatoes. Cook the mixture in oil until its raw aroma fades and the liquid reduces.
  4. Add the stock, meat, dried fish, and daddawa. Bring to a gentle simmer.
  5. Mix the groundnuts with a little warm stock until smooth, then add gradually to prevent lumps.
  6. Simmer for 15 to 20 minutes, stirring often so the soup doesn't burn. Add stock or water if it gets too thick.
  7. Add the greens near the end. Cook until tender, then adjust the seasoning.

If you use peanut butter instead of whole groundnuts, choose an unsweetened variety and start with about 1¼ cups, stirred with warm stock before adding it to the pot, then adjust to the thickness you want.

Allergy note: miyan gyada contains peanuts and is unsafe for anyone with a peanut allergy. Avoid serving it when an allergy is known or suspected, and prevent cross-contact with cooking utensils or serving dishes.

What to serve with miyan gyada

Miyan gyada is often served with tuwo, a soft grain staple shaped by hand and used to gather the soup. A familiar pairing is tuwon shinkafa, rice cooked until very soft and worked into a smooth mass. Other options include tuwon masara (maize), tuwon dawa (sorghum), tuwon alkama (wheat), eba or pounded yam, white rice, masa or waina (fermented rice cakes), and other semolina swallows. A thick miyan gyada clings naturally to tuwo; a looser version works well over rice, which absorbs the nutty sauce.

Learning Hausa through the dish

Food names preserve useful grammar. Miyar gyaɗa introduces two everyday nouns while showing how Hausa words connect within a phrase.

  • Miya: soup or sauce
  • Gyaɗa: groundnut or peanut
  • Miyar gyaɗa: groundnut soup
  • Tuwo: a thick grain-based staple
  • Shinkafa: rice
  • Nama: meat
  • Alayyahu: spinach
  • Albasa: onion
  • Barkono: pepper
  • Ruwa: water

The letter ɗ in gyaɗa represents a sound different from an ordinary d. It's often dropped in informal digital writing, which is why "gyada" appears more often online than "gyaɗa."

  • Ina son miyar gyaɗa. (I like groundnut soup.)
  • Miyar tana da daɗi. (The soup is delicious.)
  • A rage barkono, don Allah. (Please reduce the pepper.)

Conclusion

Miyan gyada shows how one familiar ingredient can carry an entire meal. Once the groundnuts are crushed and simmered, they stop behaving like a snack and become the sauce, texture, and central flavor of the pot. Meat or fish can make it more elaborate, greens and fermented seasoning add their own character, and tuwo gives the thick soup something soft and steady to hold onto. Grind the peanuts, give them time in the pot, and let one ingredient become the foundation of the meal.
